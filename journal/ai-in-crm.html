AI in CRM is aimed at the wrong job.

Most teams point it at the copy. I would point it at the decisions.

Utkarsh Som20 Oct 20269 min readCRM · AI · ML

Ask a CRM team how they use AI and you'll hear the same few answers. Faster drafts. A list of subject line options. Images on demand. I use those too. They save real time. But they're the easy part of the job, and they leave the hard part exactly where it was.

Copy was never the bottleneck

The last email that underperformed probably didn't fail on wording. It went to the wrong people, or the right people at the wrong time. Better phrasing fixes neither.

Say a shop logs 200K restock-interest signups in a year, and four out of five of those people never buy. Rewriting the email won't move that. A trigger that reaches them at the right moment might. That's a decision problem. (The numbers are made up to make the point.) The template side is in the personalisation post.

Why the decision work gets cut

A precise audience is fiddly to build: joins, time windows, exclusions, sometimes a score. Under a deadline, "opened in the last 30 days" wins because it takes two minutes. This is where AI helps. Describe who you mean in plain words and it builds conditions you can read and check. Three examples:

The ML side: models that make the call

AI doesn't have to mean a language model. The models I've built all answer the same question: who should get what. Here are three of them as small demos. The data is made up and the maths is simplified, so treat them as pictures of the idea. Fuller versions are in the projects section of my homepage.

1. Segmentation: sort customers by how they behave

Recency, frequency and spend turn a customer list into groups you can treat differently. I've used it on very large customer lists to set personalisation tiers. Move the two lines below and watch customers change tier.

2. Recommendation: pick what to show next

A hybrid recommender blends two signals: how similar a product is to what someone likes, and how popular it is overall. In an A/B test, an engine like this beat showing everyone the same products. Pick some tastes and slide the blend.

3. Churn: who's about to leave

A churn model scores how likely each customer is to go quiet, then your rules turn that score into action. I built classifiers with LightGBM and scikit-learn for this. The demo below is a simple hand-weighted score that behaves like one, so you can see the risk and its drivers move.

None of these write a word. All of them decide something. That's where I'd point AI first.

Where I'd be careful

  • It can be confidently wrong. A rule that reads well can still be built on the wrong field or the wrong time window. Read every condition.
  • Check the size before you save. If an audience is 40 people or 4 million, something is off.
  • Look at real people in it. Pull a sample and ask whether they're who you meant.
  • Mind consent. A clever segment is still only for people who've agreed to hear from you.
  • Test it against the simple version. Keep a control group. If the clever audience doesn't beat "opened in the last 30 days", you've learned something useful anyway.

If you want to try this

  • Pick one audience you've always simplified.
  • Describe it in plain language and let the tool build it.
  • Check the logic, the size and a sample of people.
  • Send it against your usual audience with a holdout.

My view is simple. If AI saves you time on copy, good. Then spend that time on the decisions, because that's where the results are. Point it at the audience first and the words second.
