Your email is either a picture or a product.

Same newsletter, built two ways. Here's what changes in the inbox.

Utkarsh Som20 Oct 20266 min readEmail · HTML/CSS

Open a promo email and try to select the headline. If nothing highlights, it's a picture of an email. Someone designed it in a graphics tool, exported one big image and dropped it in the body. In the preview it looks fine. In a real inbox it falls apart in small ways, and you usually don't hear about it.

I work in CRM, where one newsletter often goes out in several languages and currencies. That's where I started caring about this. So here's one send, built as proper HTML and CSS. You can see it below.

If we'd exported it as a single JPG it would look exactly the same in your inbox. That's why people get away with it. The difference only shows up when something goes wrong, and in email something usually does.

What happens with images off

Outlook holds images back until the reader clicks to allow them, and plenty of people never do. Try it below. Flip the switch and compare the two.

If the email is one image, the reader gets a grey box and nothing to act on. With text, they can still see what's on offer and click through.

What you get from real code

  • Prices are text. Changing a send from GBP to EUR, or English to German, means editing a line. You don't rebuild the artwork. When one campaign goes to several markets that adds up fast.
  • Buttons are separate links. You can track each one. With a single image you only know someone clicked, not on what.
  • Screen readers and translation tools work. They need real text, and a JPG gives them nothing.
  • Typos are cheap. Fix the line and resend. With an image you reopen the design file and export again.
  • Content can change per person. Names, products and offers come from your data instead of being baked into pixels.

That last point deserves its own post, so I wrote one.

Use images to support the message, not to be the message.

Two habits that help

Images still belong in email. Product shots are most of what makes a send feel like a brand. These two habits stop them from being a single point of failure.

Write alt text for every image. When images are blocked, many clients show the alt text where the image would be, so the reader still gets the point.

<img src="hero.jpg" width="700"
     alt="Summer sale: up to 50% off PC games"
     style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto">

Build the button from a table cell, not an image. A background colour on a cell renders even when images don't.

<td bgcolor="#1fd18a" style="border-radius:99px">
  <a href="https://example.com/offer"
     style="display:inline-block;padding:13px 26px;
            font:700 14px Arial,sans-serif;color:#0b0b0c;
            text-decoration:none">SHOP NOW</a>
</td>

Mind the size too

Blocked images are one way an email fails. Heavy is another. Gmail clips HTML over about 102 KB and tucks the rest behind a "View entire message" link, which can bury your footer and unsubscribe link. Big images slow the first open, mostly on mobile data. These are the limits I work to.

WhatAim forHard limit
HTML80 KB or less~102 KB
Hero image120–200 KBTry not to pass 250 KB
Product tile image40–90 KB each120 KB
Logo10–30 KB each
Social and UI icons2–8 KB each
All images togetherNear 1.0 MB1.5 MB
CSS in the style tag15 KB or less

Skip webfonts in email and let system fonts do the work. They add a download for little gain, and plenty of clients ignore them anyway.

Only the 102 KB figure is Gmail's own. The rest are targets I use. I aim for 80 KB on the HTML because your email platform rewrites every link with tracking, which makes the file longer after you've checked it. They're rules of thumb, so adjust them to your audience.

What a typical send weighs

To show the scale, here are illustrative numbers for a newsletter built with the usual mistakes. It's made up, but I've seen all of these problems in real sends.

WhatBeforeAfter a quick fix
HTML126 KB53 KB
Hero image240 KB90 KB
Product tiles (8)180–520 KB each30–60 KB each
Social icons (4)160–210 KB eachabout 1 KB each
All images together3.9 MB0.6 MB

The product tiles are usually the big one: large PNGs shown at a fraction of their size. Re-saving them as JPEGs at twice the display width keeps them sharp on high-density screens and cuts the weight to about a tenth. Small logos and social icons are the same story, like a 1080 pixel PNG shown at 30 pixels. Nothing about the design changes. The file just stops carrying pixels nobody sees.

The HTML often crosses Gmail's 102 KB line before the platform adds any tracking. Most of it is indentation and comments. Stripping them, while keeping the Outlook conditional blocks, can cut the file by more than half.

Treat the numbers as an illustration. Measure your own send, and check how the smaller versions render before you ship them.

Before you hit send

  • Headlines, prices and buttons stay as text.
  • Images carry products and mood.
  • Every image has alt text and a set width.
  • HTML under 102 KB, images as light as you can get them.
  • Send yourself a test with images off.

If your emails are one exported image today, you don't need to rebuild everything. Redo the headline and the button as text, keep the hero image, and send a test with images off. It takes a minute and you'll see the difference straight away.
